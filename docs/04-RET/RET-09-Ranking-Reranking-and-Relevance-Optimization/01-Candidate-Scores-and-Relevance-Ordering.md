# Candidate Scores and Relevance Ordering

## 1. Purpose

Retrieval systems produce candidate documents, but retrieving candidates is not the same as ordering them correctly.

A candidate may be relevant but appear too low in the result list. Another candidate may receive a high retrieval score without satisfying the user's actual information need. A ranking stage determines the order in which available candidates are presented, using signals whose meaning depends on the retrieval method, model, configuration, and data.

This chapter explains how to interpret ranking scores, reason about score comparability, combine ranking signals cautiously, establish deterministic ordering, and distinguish a ranking problem from a candidate-generation problem.

The central principle is:

> A ranking score is evidence used to order candidates. It is not automatically a measure of true relevance or a calibrated probability of usefulness.

## 2. Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

- Explain where ranking fits between candidate retrieval and final result presentation.
- Distinguish retrieval scores, ranking scores, relevance judgments, and calibrated probabilities.
- Explain why scores from different retrieval methods may not be directly comparable.
- Evaluate the assumptions behind combining multiple ranking signals.
- Calculate and trace a ranking from input scores to final ordering.
- Handle tied scores and establish deterministic tie-breaking.
- Explain why a high score does not guarantee that a result is useful.
- Distinguish ordering an existing candidate set from expanding that set.
- Implement a basic, validated ranking function in Python.
- Diagnose whether an observed problem concerns ranking or missing candidates.

## 4. Mental Model: A Score Is Evidence, Not Ground Truth

Consider a user searching an enterprise knowledge base:

> How do I rotate the credentials used by the production service?

The retrieval system returns three documents:

| Candidate | Description | Retrieval score |
|---|---|---:|
| A | Production service credential rotation procedure | 0.78 |
| B | General overview of credential management | 0.86 |
| C | Development environment password-reset guide | 0.69 |

Suppose these scores are generated by one retrieval model, where higher scores indicate a stronger match according to that model's scoring rule.

The score order is B, A, C. However, A may be the most useful document because it directly addresses production credential rotation.

The retrieval score describes the model's assessment under its own scoring mechanism. It does not independently establish whether the document satisfies the user's task.

This distinction matters because ranking systems operate with incomplete evidence:

- The query may be ambiguous.
- A document may match the query vocabulary without answering the question.
- A semantically similar document may address the wrong environment.
- The retrieval model may not represent a critical distinction.
- The score may be affected by model-specific behavior or configuration.

The engineering responsibility is not to assume that scores are truth. It is to understand what the signals represent, how they affect ordering, and where their limitations become important.

## 5. The Ranking Pipeline

A simplified retrieval pipeline can be represented as:

```text
User query
    |
    v
Candidate generation
    |
    v
Available candidate set
    |
    v
Score interpretation and ranking
    |
    v
Optional reranking
    |
    v
Final selection and presentation
```

The precise architecture depends on the application, but the responsibilities are distinct.

### 5.1 Candidate generation

Candidate generation identifies documents that may satisfy the query. It determines which documents are available for subsequent ranking.

A relevant document that never enters the candidate set cannot be moved to the top by a ranking operation over that set.

### 5.2 Ranking

Ranking orders the available candidates according to one or more signals.

A ranking stage may preserve the candidate set while changing its order. It may also transform the scores or introduce a new scoring model, depending on the architecture.

### 5.3 Reranking

Reranking applies a subsequent ranking process to an existing candidate set, often using a more expensive relevance model. Its detailed strategies and implementation belong in File 02.

### 5.4 Final result presentation

The application selects and presents results according to product requirements. The final output may also be affected by eligibility constraints, deduplication, diversity requirements, or other application-level rules.

**Engineering implication:** When results are poor, identify which stage owns the observed failure. Changing ranking weights will not recover a relevant document that was never retrieved.

## 6. Four Concepts That Must Not Be Confused

### 6.1 Retrieval score

A retrieval score is produced by a retrieval method to estimate how well a candidate matches a query according to that method.

Examples include a lexical matching score or a dense similarity score. Their numerical meanings depend on their underlying mechanisms.

A retrieval score is useful for interpreting and ordering candidates within the assumptions of its scoring method. It is not automatically comparable with scores from other methods.

### 6.2 Ranking score

A ranking score is the value used by a ranking stage to establish an ordering.

It may be:

- An original retrieval score.
- A transformed retrieval score.
- A combination of multiple signals.
- An output from a learned ranking model.
- A score produced by a subsequent reranking stage.

The score's interpretation depends on how it was generated.

### 6.3 Relevance judgment

A relevance judgment is an assessment of how well a document satisfies an information need, usually under a defined judgment procedure.

For example, a human evaluator might judge a document as highly relevant, partially relevant, or irrelevant for a particular query.

A relevance judgment and a model score are different kinds of information. The former represents an assessment under a judgment process; the latter represents a model's numerical output.

Relevance judgments can themselves be incomplete or inconsistent, so they should not be treated as infallible ground truth.

### 6.4 Calibrated probability

A calibrated probability has a probabilistic interpretation supported by calibration evidence for a defined population and task.

If a model assigns probability 0.8 to a set of outcomes, calibration would mean that approximately 80% of comparable outcomes assigned that probability occur, subject to the calibration procedure and its assumptions.

A ranking score of 0.8 does **not** automatically mean an 80% probability of relevance.

Calibration is a separate property that requires appropriate evidence. A ranking model can order results effectively without producing calibrated probabilities, and a calibrated probability is not necessarily the best ranking signal for every objective.

## 7. Ranking Signals and Their Limitations

A ranking signal is a piece of information used to determine the relative order of candidates.

Common categories include:

| Signal | Potential contribution | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Lexical score | Evidence of term matching | May reward vocabulary overlap without establishing task relevance |
| Dense similarity score | Evidence of semantic similarity | May miss exact distinctions, identifiers, or contextual constraints |
| Fused retrieval score | Combines evidence from multiple retrieval sources | Meaning depends on the fusion method and its assumptions |
| Business or application signal | Captures a product-specific preference | Can introduce bias or conflict with content relevance |
| Learned ranking score | Uses learned patterns to estimate relative usefulness | Depends on training data, objectives, and distributional assumptions |
| Reranker output | Refines ordering using a subsequent relevance model | Is constrained by the candidates supplied to it |

These signals should not be treated as interchangeable.

A lexical score may reflect term-frequency and document-statistics behavior. A dense score may reflect a similarity function. A business signal may represent a policy or product preference. Their numerical values can have different meanings even when they are all represented as floating-point numbers.

### 7.1 Signal selection is a modeling decision

Before adding a signal to a ranking function, ask:

1. What property does the signal represent?
2. Why should that property influence the order?
3. Does it contribute information that existing signals do not already provide?
4. Is its direction correct—for example, should larger or smaller values be preferred?
5. What happens when the signal is missing or unreliable?
6. Could it systematically disadvantage useful candidates?
7. How will its effect be evaluated?

A signal should not be included simply because it is available.

## 8. Score Comparability

### 8.1 Why raw scores may not be comparable

Suppose two retrieval methods return these values:

| Candidate | Method A score | Method B score |
|---|---:|---:|
| Document P | 12.4 | 0.72 |
| Document Q | 8.7 | 0.89 |

It would be incorrect to conclude that Document P is more relevant because 12.4 is numerically larger than 0.89.

The methods may use different scoring scales, distributions, directions, and meanings. A numerical comparison is meaningful only when the values have an appropriate common interpretation.

Even when two methods return values in the same interval, such as 0 to 1, the scores may not be comparable. Matching numerical ranges does not establish matching semantics.

### 8.2 Within-method ordering versus cross-method comparison

A scoring method may reliably order candidates according to its own objective while its raw values remain unsuitable for comparison with another method.

These are different questions:

- **Within-method ordering:** Which candidates does this method rank above others?
- **Cross-method comparison:** What does a score from one method mean relative to a score from another method?

The first does not automatically answer the second.

### 8.3 Normalization is not calibration

Normalization transforms values into a different scale or representation. Calibration establishes a particular interpretation, often probabilistic, supported by evidence.

For example, min-max normalization over a candidate set is:

\[
s_i' = \frac{s_i-s_{\min}}{s_{\max}-s_{\min}}
\]

where:

- \(s_i\) is the original score for candidate \(i\).
- \(s_{\min}\) is the minimum score in the chosen set.
- \(s_{\max}\) is the maximum score in that set.
- \(s_i'\) is the normalized score.

If \(s_{\max}=s_{\min}\), the denominator is zero. The implementation must define an explicit policy for this case, such as assigning the same normalized value to every candidate.

Min-max normalization can make values fit a common numerical interval. It does not prove that equal normalized values represent equal relevance, nor that differences between normalized values have the same meaning across queries.

It also depends on the candidate set: adding or removing candidates can change the normalized values of existing candidates.

**Practical rule:** Treat normalization as a transformation, not as evidence that two scoring systems have become semantically equivalent.

## 9. Combining Multiple Ranking Signals

Combining signals can be useful when different signals provide complementary evidence. However, a combination rule encodes assumptions about the meaning, scale, direction, and relative importance of those signals.

### 9.1 Weighted-sum ranking

A basic weighted sum is:

\[
S(d) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j s_j(d)
\]

where:

- \(d\) is a candidate document.
- \(s_j(d)\) is signal \(j\) for document \(d\).
- \(w_j\) is the weight assigned to signal \(j\).
- \(m\) is the number of signals.

For two signals:

\[
S(d) = 0.7s_1(d) + 0.3s_2(d)
\]

The coefficients express the intended relative contribution of the signals only under the assumptions of the chosen scales and transformations.

A signal with a much larger numerical range **can** dominate the combined score unless its weight and any transformations account for that scale difference.

For example, consider:

| Candidate | Signal A | Signal B |
|---|---:|---:|
| P | 0.80 | 10 |
| Q | 0.90 | 2 |

Using the arbitrary formula:

\[
S(d) = 0.7A(d) + 0.3B(d)
\]

we obtain:

\[
S(P)=0.7(0.80)+0.3(10)=3.56
\]

\[
S(Q)=0.7(0.90)+0.3(2)=1.23
\]

Candidate P wins because Signal B's numerical range has a substantial effect on the result. This calculation does not establish that P is more relevant. It shows the consequences of the selected formula.

Before adopting a weighted sum, establish:

- The direction and meaning of each signal.
- Whether the scales are appropriate for combination.
- Why the selected weights are justified.
- How missing or invalid signal values are handled.
- Whether the resulting order serves the intended task.
- How the design will be evaluated against a suitable baseline.

Do not present arbitrary weights as universally correct.

### 9.2 Weight interpretation

A weight is not necessarily a direct percentage of relevance.

For example, a weight of 0.7 does not guarantee that the corresponding signal contributes 70% of the practical ranking effect. Its influence also depends on the signal's distribution, range, correlations with other signals, and the behavior of the complete formula.

Changing a weight can alter the order of candidates. Its effect should be traced through actual examples rather than inferred from the coefficient alone.

### 9.3 Alternative combination approaches

Depending on the system, alternatives may include:

- Transforming signals before combining them.
- Using rank-based combination when raw score scales are unsuitable.
- Learning a ranking function from appropriate training data.
- Applying a subsequent relevance model to refine the existing ordering.

These approaches make different assumptions and introduce different costs. Their detailed algorithms are outside this file's scope. The key decision is to select a combination strategy whose assumptions fit the available evidence and application requirements.

## 10. Relevance Ordering Versus Score Magnitude

Ranking primarily concerns relative order.

Consider two result lists:

**List A**

| Rank | Document | Score |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | P | 0.92 |
| 2 | Q | 0.89 |
| 3 | R | 0.85 |

**List B**

| Rank | Document | Score |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | P | 92 |
| 2 | Q | 89 |
| 3 | R | 85 |

Both lists have the same ordering. The score magnitudes differ, but the order is identical.

Now consider a third list:

| Rank | Document | Score |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | Q | 0.94 |
| 2 | P | 0.91 |
| 3 | R | 0.85 |

The order has changed. Whether that change improves relevance depends on the task and evidence, not merely on the numerical values.

### 10.1 A higher score is not a universal guarantee

A high score can still accompany an unsuitable result when:

- The model emphasizes a feature that is not important to the task.
- The query is ambiguous.
- A document is similar in topic but wrong in context.
- The scoring function does not represent a critical requirement.
- The score comes from a different model or configuration than expected.

A ranking system should therefore be judged by whether its ordering serves the information need, not by whether its output scores look large.

### 10.2 Score differences are not automatically meaningful

A score difference of 0.05 may be important in one system and insignificant in another. Without understanding the scoring function and its behavior, the magnitude alone does not establish practical importance.

Likewise, a small difference between two scores does not necessarily mean the candidates are nearly equivalent in relevance.

When scores are close, avoid inventing a confidence interpretation unless the system provides a defensible basis for one.

## 11. Ties, Unstable Ordering, and Deterministic Tie-Breaking

### 11.1 What is a tie?

A tie occurs when two candidates have the same score under the ranking rule.

For example:

| Candidate | Score |
|---|---:|
| doc-b | 0.83 |
| doc-a | 0.83 |
| doc-c | 0.79 |

The score alone does not distinguish `doc-a` from `doc-b`.

A ranking implementation still needs a policy for producing a result list.

### 11.2 Why ordering can become unstable

If a sort operation does not define an explicit tie-breaking rule, the observed order of equal-scoring candidates may depend on upstream ordering or implementation details.

When the upstream order changes, tied candidates may appear in a different order even though their scores have not changed.

This can make debugging and result comparisons harder. It can also produce unnecessary changes in user-visible results.

### 11.3 Deterministic tie-breaking

A deterministic rule can order candidates by:

1. Descending ranking score.
2. Ascending stable document ID when scores are equal.

Formally, the ordering key is:

\[
(-S(d), \operatorname{id}(d))
\]

The negative score converts descending score order into ascending tuple order; the document ID resolves ties consistently.

For example:

| Rank | Document | Score |
|---:|---|---:|
| 1 | doc-a | 0.83 |
| 2 | doc-b | 0.83 |
| 3 | doc-c | 0.79 |

This rule is deterministic when document IDs are stable and unique.

**Important limitation:** Determinism does not establish relevance. A stable ID-based tie-breaker is a reproducibility policy, not a relevance signal.

If a product requires a meaningful preference among tied candidates, the system needs an explicit, justified secondary ranking signal instead.

## 12. Ranking an Existing Set Versus Expanding the Candidate Set

Ranking and candidate generation answer different questions.

- Candidate generation asks: **Which documents are available for consideration?**
- Ranking asks: **In what order should the available documents appear?**

Suppose the initial candidate set is:

\[
C = \{A, B, C, D\}
\]

A ranking stage can reorder these candidates, but it cannot place document E into the result list if E is absent from the input set.

This distinction leads to two common failure patterns.

### 12.1 Poor ordering

The relevant document is present but appears too low.

Example: A production credential-rotation guide is retrieved but ranked below a generic credential overview.

Potential action: Investigate the ranking signals and their interpretation, or consider a suitable subsequent ranking stage.

### 12.2 Missing candidate

The relevant document is absent from the candidate set.

Example: The production credential-rotation guide is never retrieved.

Potential action: Investigate candidate generation, eligibility constraints, or upstream retrieval behavior. Merely adjusting the ordering of existing candidates cannot recover the missing document.

These patterns can coexist. A system can have both insufficient candidate coverage and poor ordering among the candidates it retrieves.

Do not assume every poor final result is a ranking problem.

## 13. Common Score-Interpretation Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Treating every score as a probability

**Why it fails:** A ranking score may have no calibrated probabilistic meaning.

**Better approach:** Document the score's source, semantics, direction, and intended use.

### Mistake 2: Comparing scores from unrelated methods directly

**Why it fails:** Numerical values may represent different quantities or scales.

**Better approach:** Establish comparability before combining or comparing raw scores.

### Mistake 3: Assuming a common numerical range means common semantics

**Why it fails:** Two scores in the interval \([0,1]\) can have entirely different interpretations.

**Better approach:** Validate what the values represent rather than relying on their range.

### Mistake 4: Assuming weights directly express percentages of relevance

**Why it fails:** The effect of a weight depends on the signal's scale, distribution, transformation, and relationship to other signals.

**Better approach:** Trace the formula with examples and justify the combination assumptions.

### Mistake 5: Treating normalization as calibration

**Why it fails:** Rescaling values does not establish a probabilistic or cross-model interpretation.

**Better approach:** Keep scale transformation and calibration conceptually separate.

### Mistake 6: Ignoring ties

**Why it fails:** Equal scores can yield unstable ordering when the system does not specify a tie-break rule.

**Better approach:** Define and test a deterministic secondary key.

### Mistake 7: Changing ranking to fix missing candidates

**Why it fails:** A ranking stage cannot order a document it never receives.

**Better approach:** Establish whether the relevant document exists in the candidate set before changing ranking logic.

### Mistake 8: Assuming the largest score always represents the most useful result

**Why it fails:** A score reflects a model's objective and evidence, not every aspect of user intent.

**Better approach:** Inspect the relationship between the ranking signal and the actual information need.

## 14. Worked Ranking Exercise

A knowledge-base search returns four candidates for the query:

> How can I rotate credentials for a production service?

Assume the following hypothetical values have already been produced by one ranking process.

| Candidate | Description | Ranking score |
|---|---|---:|
| A | Production credential-rotation procedure | 0.82 |
| B | General credential-management overview | 0.91 |
| C | Development password-reset instructions | 0.74 |
| D | Production service deployment guide | 0.82 |

### Step 1: Apply descending score order

The initial score order is:

1. B — 0.91
2. A — 0.82
3. D — 0.82
4. C — 0.74

### Step 2: Resolve the tie

Candidates A and D have equal scores. If their stable IDs are `doc-a` and `doc-d`, respectively, ascending ID order places A before D.

The deterministic result is therefore B, A, D, C.

### Step 3: Interpret the result

The list is internally consistent with the scoring and tie-breaking rules. That does not establish that it is the best possible relevance order.

Candidate B is a general overview and might be less useful than A for the production-specific task. The ranking may be emphasizing broad topical similarity over the operational detail the user needs.

### Step 4: Choose the next engineering question

Before changing weights or replacing the ranking model, ask:

- Is the production procedure available in the candidate set?
- What does the ranking score actually represent?
- Does the score encode the distinction between production and development?
- Are the scores generated by a comparable method and configuration?
- What evidence would demonstrate that a proposed ordering change improves the task?

The answers determine whether the problem lies in candidate availability, ranking-signal interpretation, or the ranking strategy itself.

## 15. Practical Python Implementation

A basic ranking function should make its input contract explicit and produce a predictable ordering.

The implementation below:

- Accepts an iterable of candidate dictionaries.
- Requires a non-empty string `document_id`.
- Rejects duplicate document IDs.
- Requires a built-in Python `int` or `float` for `score`.
- Rejects `bool`, even though `bool` is a subclass of `int` in Python.
- Rejects non-finite floating-point scores.
- Sorts by descending score, then ascending document ID.
- Preserves top-level payload fields without mutating the input dictionaries.

### 15.1 Implementation

```python
import math
from collections.abc import Iterable
from typing import Any

def rank_candidates(
    candidates: Iterable[dict[str, Any]],
) -> list[dict[str, Any]]:
    """Rank by descending score, then ascending document ID.

    Contract:
    - score must be a built-in Python int or float.
    - bool is rejected even though it subclasses int.
    - float scores must be finite.
    - document_id must be a non-empty string and unique.
    """

    validated: list[dict[str, Any]] = []
    seen_ids: set[str] = set()

    for index, candidate in enumerate(candidates):
        if not isinstance(candidate, dict):
            raise TypeError(
                f"Candidate at index {index} must be a dictionary."
            )

        document_id = candidate.get("document_id")
        if not isinstance(document_id, str) or not document_id.strip():
            raise ValueError(
                f"Candidate at index {index} has an invalid document_id."
            )

        if document_id in seen_ids:
            raise ValueError(
                f"Duplicate document_id: {document_id!r}"
            )

        score = candidate.get("score")
        if isinstance(score, bool) or type(score) not in (int, float):
            raise TypeError(
                f"Candidate {document_id!r} must have a built-in "
                "int or float score."
            )

        if isinstance(score, float) and not math.isfinite(score):
            raise ValueError(
                f"Candidate {document_id!r} has a non-finite score."
            )

        seen_ids.add(document_id)
        validated.append(dict(candidate))

    return sorted(
        validated,
        key=lambda candidate: (
            -candidate["score"],
            candidate["document_id"],
        ),
    )
```

The explicit type check is intentionally narrow. It rejects `bool`, `decimal.Decimal`, and third-party numeric scalar types that are not built-in `int` or `float` instances of the exact types specified by this contract. A system that needs to support other numeric types should define and test a broader numeric conversion policy rather than silently assuming all numeric-like objects behave identically.

The finite-value check rejects `float("nan")`, positive infinity, and negative infinity. Such values can undermine intuitive score ordering or create inconsistent behavior across ranking implementations.

Python integers are arbitrary precision and are not subject to floating-point finiteness checks. The code does not attempt to impose an application-specific score range because that range depends on the scoring method.

### 15.2 Example

```python
candidates = [
    {"document_id": "doc-b", "score": 0.82, "source": "dense"},
    {"document_id": "doc-a", "score": 0.91, "source": "lexical"},
    {"document_id": "doc-c", "score": 0.82, "source": "dense"},
]

ranked = rank_candidates(candidates)

for rank, candidate in enumerate(ranked, start=1):
    print(rank, candidate["document_id"], candidate["score"])
```

Expected output:

```text
1 doc-a 0.91
2 doc-b 0.82
3 doc-c 0.82
```

The equal-scoring candidates are ordered by document ID.

### 15.3 Complexity

Let \(n\) be the number of candidates.

- Validation and copying take \(O(n)\) time, assuming bounded-size candidate records and ordinary average-case set operations.
- Sorting takes \(O(n \log n)\) time in the general case.
- The validation set and copied candidate list require \(O(n)\) additional space, excluding the variable-sized payloads contained in candidate dictionaries.

Copying each dictionary is a shallow copy. Nested objects remain shared with the input. The implementation avoids mutating the input dictionaries, but it does not deep-copy or make nested payloads immutable.

### 15.4 Production limitations

This example demonstrates ordering, validation, and deterministic tie-breaking. It is not a complete production ranking service.

A production implementation may also need to define:

- The source and version of each score.
- Score direction and transformations.
- Missing-score behavior.
- Schema validation for candidate payloads.
- Limits on candidate count and record size.
- Logging and error-handling policy.
- Observability for score distributions and unexpected ordering.
- A policy for ties when document IDs are not suitable product-level tie-breakers.

Do not add these behaviors without defining the corresponding system requirements.

## 16. Interview Preparation

### 16.1 Conceptual questions

**Q1. What is the difference between a ranking score and relevance?**

**Answer:** A ranking score is a model- or rule-produced value used to order candidates. Relevance concerns how well a candidate satisfies the information need. The score is evidence about relevance under the scoring method's assumptions; it is not relevance itself.

**Q2. Why are scores from two retrieval methods not necessarily comparable?**

**Answer:** They may be generated by different mathematical mechanisms, optimized for different objectives, and distributed across different numerical ranges. A direct numerical comparison is meaningful only if the values have a justified common interpretation.

**Q3. Does normalizing two score distributions make them comparable?**

**Answer:** Not necessarily. Normalization changes numerical representation, but does not establish semantic equivalence or calibration. A common range is not proof that equal values have equal relevance meaning.

**Q4. Why should a ranking system define a tie-breaking rule?**

**Answer:** A tie-break rule makes ordering predictable when the primary scores are equal. Stable ordering helps reproducibility and debugging, but the tie-breaker does not itself establish relevance.

### 16.2 Comparison and trade-off questions

**Q5. When might a weighted sum be reasonable, and what must be established first?**

**Answer:** It can be reasonable when the signals provide useful complementary evidence and their scales, directions, and relative contributions are understood. The team must justify the weights, transformations, missing-value policy, and intended ranking behavior. The formula should be evaluated against an appropriate baseline rather than accepted because the weights look intuitive.

**Q6. Why might a rank-based combination be preferable to a raw-score weighted sum?**

**Answer:** Rank-based combination can avoid directly combining raw values whose scales differ substantially. However, it discards some information about score magnitude and has its own assumptions. It should be selected based on the task and evidence, not as a universal replacement for score-based methods.

### 16.3 Scenario-based question

**Q7. Users report that a generic overview frequently appears above a document that contains the exact procedure they need. What would you investigate?**

**Answer:**

1. Confirm that the exact procedure is present in the candidate set.
2. Inspect the score source and the ordering rule.
3. Determine whether the signals distinguish exact operational relevance from general topical similarity.
4. Check whether scores are being combined under defensible scale and weight assumptions.
5. Trace representative examples to identify why the generic overview wins.
6. Choose a targeted ranking change only after identifying the cause.
7. Validate the change using a suitable evaluation process rather than assuming a more intuitive ordering is necessarily better.

If the exact procedure is absent, the problem is upstream of ordering. A ranking adjustment alone cannot recover it.

### 16.4 Practical tracing question

**Q8. Three candidates have the following scores: A = 0.7, B = 0.9, C = 0.7. What is the deterministic order if ties are resolved by ascending document ID and the IDs are `doc-a`, `doc-b`, and `doc-c`?**

**Answer:** B, A, C. B has the highest score. A and C have equal scores, so ascending document ID places A before C.

### 16.5 Advanced follow-up

**Q9. A ranking change produces larger scores but leaves the order unchanged. Has relevance improved?**

**Answer:** The score magnitudes alone do not establish improvement. If the ordering is unchanged, the displayed order may be unchanged as well, but that still does not prove relevance was already optimal or that the score transformation is useful. The engineering team must understand the transformation and assess the resulting behavior using suitable evidence.

## 17. Common Misconceptions

- **“The highest score is the most relevant document.”** The highest score is the winner under the scoring rule. Whether that winner is truly the most useful result is a separate question.
- **“Scores between zero and one are probabilities.”** A bounded numerical range does not imply calibration.
- **“A weight of 0.7 means a signal contributes 70% of relevance.”** A weight's practical effect depends on the formula, scales, and distributions.
- **“Normalization solves every score-comparability problem.”** Normalization changes scale; it does not automatically align meaning.
- **“Deterministic ranking guarantees correct ranking.”** Determinism guarantees repeatable behavior under the same inputs and rule, not relevance.
- **“A better ranking can recover every missed document.”** Ranking cannot recover candidates absent from the input set.
- **“A larger score difference always means a larger relevance difference.”** Score magnitude has no universal relevance interpretation.

## 19. Key Takeaways and Next Step

Ranking is the process of ordering the candidates available to a system. The scores used to create that order are signals whose meaning depends on their generation method and assumptions.

The most important engineering distinctions are:

- A score is not automatically a relevance judgment or probability.
- Raw scores from different methods are not necessarily comparable.
- Normalization does not establish calibration.
- Combining signals requires explicit assumptions about scale, direction, and contribution.
- Deterministic tie-breaking improves repeatability but does not prove relevance.
- Ranking changes cannot recover candidates that are missing from the candidate set.
